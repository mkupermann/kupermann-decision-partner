# Case 1: pilot rollout

**Recommendation: do not approve full rollout on this evidence. Continue with a bounded validation of routine-ticket use before making a spending decision.**

The measured saving is **2 minutes net per ticket**, after the additional 4 minutes of review. If that result transfers to all 14,400 routine tickets per year:

- Capacity released: 480 staff hours per year.
- Value at the stated loaded rate: EUR 24,000 per year.
- After the annual licence: EUR 6,000 per year.
- First year, including integration: **EUR 6,000 negative**.

These are extrapolations, not demonstrated business benefits. Released capacity becomes cash savings only if staffing expenditure actually falls; otherwise its value depends on useful redeployment. Training, administration and other unreported costs would reduce the benefit. Even under the stated assumptions, simple integration payback is two years.

Eight experienced volunteers choosing their own tickets do not establish representative productivity or team-wide adoption. Their results may reflect experience, enthusiasm and easier ticket selection. Complex tickets remain untested and should stay outside any proposed initial scope.

Two of 80 drafts contained incorrect cancellation terms. Review caught both, which supports retaining review, but does not establish a reliable future escape rate. The business has not defined acceptable quality. Nor can we assess the requested 30% productivity improvement without a comparable baseline handling time and a defined measure of productivity.

**Next action:** prepare a short validation protocol and decision paper for the accountable sponsor. Before testing, agree the quality threshold, productivity measure and investment hurdle. Use a representative range of staff and routine tickets, count all review and rework time, check final-answer quality, and measure actual use. Determine sample size from the chosen quality and benefit thresholds. Present the current first-year shortfall alongside the uncertainties. I can recommend this work; spending approval remains with the board or delegated owner.

# Case 2: conflicting priorities

**Provisionally choose B, provided an eight-week deployment is acceptable.** It meets the same technical and security requirements while saving EUR 15,000 annually. There is currently no agreed deadline or quantified benefit that justifies paying A's premium.

This recommendation assumes cost takes priority when the value of earlier delivery is unproven. It is not proof that the six-week delay has no cost.

| Provider | Annual cost | Deployment |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | EUR 40,000 | 2 weeks |
| B | EUR 25,000 | 8 weeks |

**An objective weighted ranking is not available from these facts.** Weights express priorities, and those priorities have not been agreed. Supplying my own weights and presenting the resulting number as objective would misrepresent the decision. A and B each win on one criterion; neither dominates the other.

A useful numeric decision boundary is the **EUR 15,000 annual premium for six weeks of earlier deployment**. For a one-year comparison, A needs more than EUR 15,000 of incremental benefit from earlier availability to justify that premium—equivalent to EUR 2,500 per week if the benefit is spread evenly across those six weeks. If the premium continues for several years, compare costs and benefits over that actual commitment period.

**Next action:** establish whether eight weeks meets the operational need and estimate the consequence of waiting those extra six weeks. Keep B if the delay is acceptable and its cost does not outweigh the relevant premium. Choose A if an essential deadline or sufficiently valuable earlier availability justifies the additional expense. Once those preferences are explicit, a weighted score can document them, but cannot make them objective. No purchase is authorized or made.
