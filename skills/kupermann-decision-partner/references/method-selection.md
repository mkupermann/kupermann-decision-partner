# Select the method that can change the decision

Use the least elaborate method that addresses the material uncertainty. Combining methods is useful when they answer different questions. A longer process is not inherently more rigorous.

| Signal | Appropriate starting point | Useful output | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unclear decision or ambiguous terms | Restate the problem, identify unknowns and constraints, split independent questions | A decision sentence and a small list of unknowns | Do not optimise a solution before agreeing the actual problem |
| No plausible approach | Analogy, simpler case, decomposition or working backward | A candidate route and the conditions under which the analogy holds | Similarity is a hypothesis, not proof |
| Competing legitimate goals | Explicit criteria and an options comparison | Trade-offs, decision boundary, conditional recommendation | Weights express values; ask their owner or label illustrative assumptions |
| A factual claim drives the choice | Original record, measurement, reproduction or qualified source | A traceable claim and the scope of the check | Match the evidence to the claim; counting sources is insufficient |
| Claimed improvement over current practice | Comparable baseline and defined outcome | Absolute adequacy and incremental benefit reported separately | A before/after change alone may have other causes |
| Causal effect matters | A suitable controlled design, with expertise proportional to stakes | Effect estimate with uncertainty and validity limits | Randomisation is not always feasible; name confounders rather than hiding them |
| Outcome depends on uncertain inputs | Sensitivity analysis or a small set of meaningful scenarios | Values at which the preferred option changes | Do not invent precise distributions for unknown quantities |
| A preferred option appears inevitable | Strongest competing explanation and prospective failure analysis | A plausible failure mechanism and an early signal | A dramatic story alone is weak evidence |
| More research is being requested | Compare the likely value of new information with delay and investigation cost | A stopping rule or a specific next test | Use qualitative judgment if credible values are unavailable |
| Evidence has changed | Revise the affected assumption and recompute the comparison | Versioned recommendation and reason for revision | Sunk drafting effort does not support the earlier recommendation |

## Criteria that do not move after the result

Write the acceptable outcome before a test. If different outcomes receive different labels, make the categories mutually exclusive and complete. Include an inconclusive outcome where measurement or sample size can prevent a decision.

An experiment may need a negative control to detect bias in the measurement system. A business comparison usually needs a realistic alternative. These serve different purposes. If an inexpensive alternative meets the requirement, its success may remove the case for the expensive option.

## Make comparisons commensurate

Check population, measurement interval, units and included costs. Distinguish net from gross benefit, recurring from one-off cost, and a capability from realised use. Released staff time is capacity; useful redeployment can add operational value; cash savings require reduced expenditure. Useful redeployment alone does not establish cash savings. Keep a range or conditional result when the unknown input dominates the answer.

For example, a provider available six weeks earlier for an additional annual fee cannot be selected by declaring arbitrary weights objective. Show the premium, identify the decision horizon, and ask whether the benefit of earlier availability exceeds it.

## Finish at the right depth

A reversible agenda change needs an agenda. A consequential supplier choice needs explicit criteria and a record of unresolved trade-offs. A causal claim about a pilot needs evidence capable of addressing causation. Do not force all three through an identical checklist or demand the same quantity of documentation.
